### 
Facts
On the late afternoon of January 30, 1984, a wedding celebration was taking place in Balinciagao, Pasil, Kalinga-Apayao. The victim, Elpidio Dalsen (Provincial Development Officer of Kalinga-Apayao), was resting and asleep inside the house of his parents. 

Witness Beatrice Canao saw the accused-appellant, Bonifacio Balansi alias "Ban-os" (a Barangay Captain and member of the Civilian Home Defense Force [CHDF]), standing armed with a Garand rifle by the entrance of the house. Moments after Canao exited the house, two gunshots rang out. She then saw Balansi descending the stairs. Another witness, Yulo Asbok, encountered Balansi coming down with the rifle and grappled with him for possession of the firearm. Balansi fled the scene and hid for four days before surrendering. Inside the second floor of the house, Dalsen was found dead with fatal gunshot wounds. 

The defense claimed that Balansi was only investigating the commotion in his capacity as a CHDF member, did not shoot the victim, and fled because he was being blamed. 

The Regional Trial Court convicted Balansi of Murder qualified by treachery, evident premeditation, and taking advantage of superior strength/means to weaken the defense, sentencing him to death and ordering him to pay ₱590,000.00 in damages. 


### Issues
1.	Whether circumstantial evidence was sufficient to prove the guilt of the accused beyond reasonable doubt. 

2.	Whether the killing was attended by treachery and evident premeditation to qualify the crime to Murder. 

3.	Whether the generic aggravating circumstance of dwelling (morada) should be appreciated, considering the victim was killed in the house of his parents and not his own separate dwelling. 

### Ruling
1. Sufficiency of Circumstantial Evidence
Yes. Under Section 4 (now Section 5), Rule 133 of the Rules of Court, circumstantial evidence is sufficient for conviction if:


1.	There is more than one circumstance;

2.	The facts from which the inferences are derived are proven; and

3.	The combination of all the circumstances is such as to produce a conviction beyond reasonable doubt. 

Although no eyewitness saw the actual pulling of the trigger: 


•	Balansi was seen holding a long rifle outside the door right before the shooting; 

•	Gunshots rang out immediately thereafter; 

•	He was seen descending the stairs from the second floor where the victim lay dead; 

•	A witness wrestled the rifle from him at the scene; and 

•	He fled and hid for four days.

Flight is a silent admission of guilt ("The wicked flee when no man pursueth, but the righteous are bold as a lion"), rebutting his claim of innocent official investigation. 


2. Absence of Qualifying Circumstances (Treachery and Evident Premeditation)
No qualifying circumstances were proven.


•	Treachery: Cannot be presumed. It must be proven as conclusively as the crime itself. Because no eyewitness testified to the exact manner in which the attack commenced, treachery cannot be appreciated merely from the fact that the victim was shot. 

•	Evident Premeditation: There was no proof of the time when the offender determined to commit the crime, an act manifestly indicating that he clung to his determination, and a sufficient lapse of time between determination and execution to allow for reflection. 

Consequently, the crime committed is only Homicide, not Murder.


3. Appreciation of Dwelling as an Aggravating Circumstance
Yes. Even if the house belonged to the victim's parents, dwelling is aggravating. 

The Court reaffirmed the doctrine in People v. Basa (83 Phil. 622): the rationale behind dwelling as an aggravating circumstance is the violation of the sanctity of privacy and peace that the law accords to a human abode. One does not have to be the titled owner or exclusive tenant; an invited guest or a relative sleeping in the house of another enjoys the protection and sanctuary of that roof. The intruder who enters to kill commits a greater breach of social peace and demonstrates greater perversity. 


### Dispositive Portion
The Supreme Court AFFIRMED WITH MODIFICATION the conviction, finding Bonifacio Balansi guilty of Homicide aggravated by dwelling (with no mitigating circumstances). He was sentenced to an indeterminate penalty of 8 years and 1 day of prisión mayor (as minimum) to 17 years, 4 months, and 1 day of reclusión temporal (as maximum), and ordered to pay the awarded damages. 

Why did the Supreme Court appreciate the aggravating circumstance of disregard of dwelling to a mere house guest?
1. The Protection Attaches to the Sanctuary of the Structure
The aggravating circumstance of dwelling does not depend on strict legal title, ownership, or permanent residency. Rather, it is anchored on the inviolability of the place as a sanctuary of shelter and repose:
•	A dwelling is defined broadly in penal jurisprudence as the building or structure exclusively used for rest, comfort, and privacy.
•	When an occupant or host opens their home to a guest, that sanctuary and the expectation of safety and peace extend to everyone legitimately sheltered under that roof.
•	The offender demonstrates an enhanced perversity and disrespect for the peace of the home by breaching that sanctuary, regardless of whether the target of the assault is the homeowner or an invited guest staying or resting therein.

2. Settled Jurisprudential Precedent
The doctrine applied in People v. Balansi and similar cases traces back to landmark rulings affirming that temporary presence inside a house does not strip a victim of the protection of morada:
•	People v. Basa (83 Phil. 622) / People v. Alcala (46 Phil. 739): The Supreme Court expressly recognized that dwelling is aggravating when the victim is a guest in another person’s house. The Court reasoned that an invited visitor enjoys the peace and protection of the host’s roof; invading that peace violates the very principle of sanctuary underlying Article 14, paragraph 3.
•	People v. Ambis (68 Phil. 645): The circumstance was appreciated where the victim was sleeping as an invited guest in the house of a relative. The Court stressed that one does not need to be the owner or tenant of the house to be covered by the circumstance of dwelling, provided the victim is lawfully residing or staying there at the time of the commission of the crime.

3. Firing From Outside Into the House
In Balansi, this principle was coupled with the doctrine on firing from the exterior:
•	The shooter remained in the dark outside and fired through the window into the interior where the victim was seated conversing.
•	The Court reiterated that physical entry across the threshold is not necessary. Firing into the interior of a house breaches the quiet and security of the domicile just as effectively as stepping inside. Because the victim was peacefully seated inside a private home with his hosts, the perversity of violating that domestic sanctuary attended the killing.
Key Requisite: Absence of Provocation
The only limitation where dwelling cannot be appreciated against an offender attacking a guest (or anyone inside) is when the victim gives immediate and sufficient provocation from within the dwelling. In Balansi, the victim was merely engaged in peaceful conversation inside his relative's home and offered no provocation to the assailant outside, making the appreciation of dwelling legally proper.
