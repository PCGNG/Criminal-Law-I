Facts:
In January 1986, during the final months of the Marcos administration, President Ferdinand E. Marcos issued a direct verbal order, followed by a written presidential memorandum, to Luis Tabuena, the General Manager of the Manila International Airport Authority (MIAA). Tabuena was instructed to immediately withdraw and deliver ₱55 million from MIAA funds to the Office of the President to liquidate outstanding obligations incurred by the Philippine National Construction Corporation (PNCC).
To execute this directive:
1.	Tabuena instructed Adolfo Peralta, MIAA Acting Finance Services Manager, to prepare the disbursements.
2.	In three separate transactions in January 1986, MIAA checks totaling ₱55 million were drawn against MIAA accounts at the Philippine National Bank (PNB).
3.	The checks were encashed, and the physical cash was transported in duffel bags to Malacañang Palace, where Tabuena personally handed the money over to Mrs. Fe Roa Gimenez, private secretary to President Marcos.
4.	Tabuena received only informal receipts/slips from Gimenez acknowledging receipt of the cash, but obtained no standard government vouchers or documentation showing how the funds were subsequently disbursed.
Following the 1986 EDSA Revolution, state auditors discovered that the ₱55 million withdrawal bypassed standard government accounting and auditing procedures under the Government Auditing Code (P.D. 1445).
The Ombudsman charged Tabuena and Peralta before the Sandiganbayan with three counts of Malversation of Public Funds under Article 217 of the Revised Penal Code (RPC).
The Sandiganbayan convicted both accused of malversation, holding that the disbursement completely ignored standard COA regulations, lacked proof of actual payment to PNCC, and that Tabuena could not seek refuge under presidential orders. Tabuena and Peralta appealed to the Supreme Court.
Issue: 
Are the accused entitled to the justifying circumstance of acting in obedience to an order issued by a superior for a lawful purpose (Article 11, paragraph 6 of the RPC), or the defense of good faith (mistake of fact)?
Ruling: 
Obedience to Superior Orders and Mistake of Fact
The Supreme Court ruled that Tabuena acted under the justifying circumstance of obedience to an order issued by a superior for a lawful purpose (Article 11, paragraph 6, RPC), coupled with the doctrine of mistake of fact (ignorantia facti excusat under People v. Oanis and United States v. Ah Chong):
•	Order from a Superior: The directive came directly from President Marcos, who exercised ultimate executive control and supervision over all government agencies, including MIAA.
•	Apparent Lawful Purpose: Marcos directed that the funds be used to pay government obligations to PNCC. Under the legal framework at the time (specifically P.D. 1953 and related presidential issuances), the President exercised broad powers to reallocate public funds and direct government corporate revenues toward national infrastructure obligations.
•	Good Faith Reliance: Tabuena acted under the honest belief that he was duty-bound to execute the command of the Chief Executive. Even if the underlying presidential order was irregular or legally flawed, Tabuena complied in good faith, unaware of any unlawful scheme. Disobedience to a direct presidential directive carried significant professional and personal peril at the time.

Ratio Decidendi:

Dispositive:
The Supreme Court REVERSED the Sandiganbayan decision and ACQUITTED Luis Tabuena and Adolfo Peralta of all charges.
Question:
Why did the Supreme Court rule that Tabuena is not criminally liable?
Answer:
1. Absence of Criminal Intent (Animus Lucrandi)
Malversation of public funds under Article 217 of the Revised Penal Code is an intentional felony requiring dolo—specifically criminal intent and the personal misappropriation or conversion of public funds.
•	Tabuena did not pocket, retain, or divert a single centavo of the ₱55 million for his personal benefit.
•	The funds were physically delivered in cash directly to the Office of the President through President Marcos’s private secretary, Fe Roa Gimenez, strictly pursuant to executive instructions.
•	The subsequent failure of Malacañang or PNCC to properly account for or liquidate the funds could not be retroactively imputed to Tabuena as personal misappropriation.
2. Obedience to an Order Issued by a Superior for a Lawful Purpose (Art. 11, Par. 6, RPC)
The Court found that Tabuena acted under the justifying circumstance provided in Article 11, paragraph 6 of the Revised Penal Code:
•	Order from a Superior: The directive originated directly from the President of the Philippines, who held ultimate executive control and administrative supervision over MIAA.
•	Apparent Lawful Purpose: President Marcos stated that the funds were needed to pay outstanding government corporate obligations to the Philippine National Construction Corporation (PNCC). Given the extensive decree-making powers and fund-reallocation authority exercised by the President at the time (e.g., under P.D. 1953), the directive appeared legitimate and regular on its face.
•	Presumption of Regularity: Subordinate officers are entitled to rely on the presumption that orders from the Chief Executive are issued in regular performance of official functions unless patently illegal.
3. Defense of Good Faith and Mistake of Fact (Ignorantia Facti Excusat)
Applying the doctrines in People v. Ah Chong and People v. Oanis, the Court held that even if the presidential directive was legally flawed or disguised an illicit disbursement, Tabuena acted under an honest mistake of fact:
•	He entertained a good-faith belief that he was duty-bound to obey the order of the President.
•	In January 1986, defying a direct written and verbal presidential command carried grave personal and professional hazards.
•	His compliance without fraudulent intent or knowledge of any diversion negated criminal liability.
