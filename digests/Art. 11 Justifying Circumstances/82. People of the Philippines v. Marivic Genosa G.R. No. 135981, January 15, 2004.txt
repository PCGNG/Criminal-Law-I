### Fact
Marivic Genosa and Ben Genosa were married in 1989. Throughout their marriage, Ben habitually engaged in excessive drinking, compulsive gambling, and severe domestic abuse. Marivic suffered chronic battery, verbal degradation, and physical assaults, frequently requiring medical treatment and once prompting her to secure an unheeded barangay protection conciliation.

On the evening of November 15, 1995, Ben arrived home intoxicated and began a violent confrontation with Marivic, who was eight months pregnant. Ben repeatedly struck, kicked, dragged, and choked her, and even cut her with a broken beer bottle. When Ben briefly stopped to lie down on their bed, Marivic—fearing for her life and the unborn child—retrieved a .38 caliber revolver from a drawer and shot Ben in the head while he was in bed. Ben died instantly.

Marivic was charged with Parricide before the Regional Trial Court (RTC) of Ormoc City. The RTC convicted her of parricide qualified by treachery and sentenced her to death. On automatic review, the Supreme Court remanded the case to receive expert psychiatric testimony on whether Marivic suffered from Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS). Clinical psychologists Dr. Alfredo Pajarillo and Dr. Zeny S. Camacho confirmed that Marivic suffered from the psychological and behavioral characteristics of BWS.

### Issue
1.	Does Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS) qualify as a valid form of self-defense under Article 11, paragraph 1 of the Revised Penal Code (RPC)?
2.	Did Marivic Genosa establish all the legal requisites of self-defense under the RPC?

### Ruling
1. Recognition of Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS)
The Supreme Court formally recognized Battered Woman Syndrome in Philippine jurisprudence. The Court adopted Lenore Walker’s formulation of the cycle of violence, consisting of three distinct phases:
•	The Tension-Building Phase: Minor battering incidents, friction, and psychological strain.
•	The Acute Battering Incident: The eruption of acute physical, sexual, or emotional violence.
•	The Tranquil, Loving (or Contrite) Phase: Remorse, promises of reform, and temporary calm.
The Court held that repeated exposure to this cyclical trauma causes a profound psychological state known as learned helplessness, where the victim develops an acute sensitivity to imminent danger and perceives killing the batterer as the only viable means of self-preservation.

2. Incomplete Justification: Absence of Imminent or Actual Unlawful Aggression
Despite recognizing BWS, the Court ruled that Marivic could not be completely acquitted on the ground of self-defense under Article 11(1) of the RPC because the indispensable element of unlawful aggression was missing at the precise time of the shooting:
•	Unlawful aggression requires an actual, sudden, unexpected attack or an immediate, imminent threat of harm.
•	While Ben had brutally battered Marivic earlier that evening, the acute battering incident had already ceased. At the time Marivic pulled the trigger, Ben was asleep or lying down in bed.
•	Under the strict framework of the Revised Penal Code, the threat was neither present nor imminent at the exact moment of the fatal shot. Consequently, self-defense failed as a complete justifying circumstance.

Legislative Aftermath: Republic Act No. 9262
The decision prompted the Philippine Congress to pass Republic Act No. 9262 (Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004), signed into law just two months later on March 8, 2004.
Section 26 of R.A. 9262 codified BWS as an explicit statutory defense, modifying the classical RPC rule applied in Genosa:
"Victim-survivors who are found by the courts to be suffering from battered woman syndrome do not incur any criminal and civil liability notwithstanding the absence of any of the elements for justifying circumstances of self-defense under the Revised Penal Code."
Under R.A. 9262, the absence of instantaneous unlawful aggression at the exact time of the assault no longer bars a battered woman from complete exoneration.

### Ratio
Allegedly, there are four characteristics of the syndrome: 
(1) the woman believes that the violence was her fault; 
(2) she has an inability to place the responsibility for the violence elsewhere; 
(3) she fears for her life and/or her children's lives; and 
(4) she has an irrational belief that the abuser is omnipresent and omniscient.  
Living in constant danger of harm or death, she knows that future beatings are almost certain to occur and will escalate over time. Her intimate knowledge of the violent nature of her batterer makes her alert to when a particular attack is forthcoming, and when it will seriously threaten her survival. Trapped in a cycle of violence and constant fear, it is not unlikely that she would succumb to her helplessness and fail to perceive possible solutions to the problem other than to injure or kill her batterer. She is seized by fear of an existing or impending lethal aggression and thus would have no opportunity beforehand to deliberate on her acts and to choose a less fatal means of eliminating her sufferings.

"The basic principle in our criminal law is that a person is criminally liable for a felony committed by him. Under the classical theory on which our penal code is mainly based, the basis of criminal liability is human free will. Man is essentially a moral creature with an absolutely free will to choose between good and evil. When he commits a felonious or criminal act (delito doloso), the act is presumed to have been done voluntarily, i.e., with freedom, intelligence and intent. Man, therefore, should be adjudged or held accountable for wrongful acts so long as free will appears unimpaired."
 
In the instant case, it is equally important to determine whether Appellant Genosa had acted freely, intelligently and voluntarily when she killed her spouse. The Court, however, cannot properly evaluate her battered-woman-syndrome defense, absent expert testimony on her mental and emotional state at the time of the killing and the possible psychological cause and effect of her fatal act. Unlike in Parazo, we cannot simply refer her for proper psychological or psychiatric examination and thereafter admit the findings and evaluation as part of the records of the cases for purposes of automatic review. The prosecution has likewise the right to a fair trial, which includes the opportunity to cross-examine the defense witnesses and to refute the expert opinion given. Thus, consistent with the principle of due process, a partial reopening of the case is apropos, so as to allow the defense the opportunity to present expert evidence consistent with our foregoing disquisition, as well as the prosecution the opportunity to cross examine and refute the same.

### Dispositive:
The Supreme Court downgraded the penalty from death to an indeterminate sentence of six (6) years and one (1) day of prision mayor as minimum, to fourteen (14) years, eight (8) months and one (1) day of reclusion temporal as maximum.
Because Marivic had already been detained beyond the minimum term of her penalty and had exhibited good conduct, the Court ordered her immediate release from custody, provided she was not held for any other lawful cause.


### Question:
Why did the court rule that Marivic Genosa is not afflicted with the battered woman syndrome?

Answer:
While the Supreme Court formally recognized the concept of Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS) in Philippine jurisprudence, it held that the defense failed to conclusively prove that Marivic Genosa was afflicted with BWS at the legal standard required to sustain self-defense.

The Court reached this conclusion based on the following specific legal and evidentiary grounds:
1. Failure to Prove the Full Three-Phase "Cycle of Violence"
The Court adopted Dr. Lenore Walker’s classic psychological formulation, ruling that for a woman to be legally classified as suffering from Battered Woman Syndrome, the couple must have gone through the cycle of violence at least twice: 
1.	The Tension-Building Phase 
2.	The Acute Battering Incident 
3.	The Tranquil, Loving, or Non-Violent (Contrite) Phase 
While the evidence showed that Ben repeatedly assaulted and abused Marivic (establishing acute battering incidents), the defense fell short of proving that their relationship exhibited all three recurring phases, particularly the contrite/tranquil phase. The Court observed that the testimony failed to establish a documented recurring cycle where severe violence was followed by genuine remorse, tender reconciliations, and the cyclical dependency that induces the psychological trap of learned helplessness. 
2. Insufficiency of the Expert Testimonies
Although two expert witnesses—clinical psychologists Dr. Alfredo Pajarillo and Dr. Zeny Camacho—testified that Marivic exhibited symptoms of BWS, the Court found critical deficiencies in their factual foundation:
•	Timing of Evaluations: The psychiatric and psychological examinations took place years after the incident and trial had already concluded, rather than contemporaneous with the event.
•	One-Sided Information: The expert assessments were based almost exclusively on self-serving narrations provided by Marivic herself and her immediate relatives, with little independent, objective corroboration of the couple's day-to-day relational dynamics over the years.
•	Absence of Diagnostic Baseline: The experts did not sufficiently map Marivic's history onto the specific cyclical progression required by the scientific definition adopted by the Court.
3. Insufficiency to Support Complete Self-Defense
The Court emphasized that even if a woman experiences severe spousal abuse, the existence of BWS does not operate as a blanket license to kill. Under the Revised Penal Code: 
•	A valid claim of self-defense requires unlawful aggression that is actual, sudden, unexpected, or imminent. 
•	According to Marivic's own testimony, after the violent altercation that evening, Ben ceased his assault and fell asleep. 
•	Because there was a sufficient time interval during which Marivic withdrew to another room before returning to shoot him while he was lying down, the imminent threat had ceased. 
How the Abuse Was Treated by the Court
While the Court ruled that Marivic failed to meet the strict legal threshold for BWS to justify an acquittal under self-defense, it did not disregard the abuse. Instead, it treated the cumulative trauma and battery as mitigating circumstances: 
1.	Psychological illness / Diminished willpower (analogous under Art. 13[9] and Art. 13[10] of the RPC), finding that the repeated beatings produced an acute state of psychological paralysis; and
2.	Passion and obfuscation (Art. 13[6], RPC), arising from the violent beating she endured that same night while heavily pregnant.
These mitigating factors eliminated any aggravating circumstance, resulting in a significantly reduced sentence and her subsequent release on credit for time served.
