92. People of the Philippines v. Roy San Gaspar, G.R. No. 180496, April 2, 2014
Facts:
Late at night, Roy arrived home and found his wife, Imelda, asleep on the floor alongside her two minor children. Angered because the door was not opened for him immediately and displeased that Imelda was sleeping beside the children, Roy kicked her. A verbal confrontation ensued. Roy then went upstairs, retrieved a loaded 12-gauge shotgun, lit a kerosene lamp near the bedroom door, aimed the firearm at Imelda, and shot her directly in the head in full view of the children before fleeing the scene. Imelda sustained a fatal gunshot wound on the left frontal aspect of her forehead surrounded by gunpowder burns. 

When prosecuted for parricide, Roy invoked the exempting circumstance of accident under Article 12, paragraph 4 of the Revised Penal Code. He claimed that he had no intention of shooting his wife and that the shotgun discharged purely by accident when he pushed the bedroom door.
Issue:
Does the exempting circumstance of accident under Article 12, paragraph 4 of the Revised Penal Code exempt Roy from criminal liability?
Ruling: 
Under Article 12, paragraph 4 of the Revised Penal Code, any person who, while performing a lawful act with due care, causes an injury by mere accident without fault or intention of causing it, is exempt from criminal liability.
For accident to be appreciated as an exempting circumstance, the following elements must concur:
1.	The accused was performing a lawful act;
2.	With due care;
3.	The injury caused to another was by mere accident; and
4.	Without fault or intention of causing the injury.
In rejecting the defense of accident, the Supreme Court relied on the following findings and principles:
•	Inconsistency with Physical and Medical Evidence: The physical evidence demonstrated a frontal gunshot wound to the forehead with gunpowder tattooing inflicted at a close range of no more than six feet. This trajectory and close-range burn refuted Roy's assertion that the shotgun dropped or discharged spontaneously upon pushing the door, demonstrating instead a direct and deliberate firing. 
•	Absence of Performance of a Lawful Act with Due Care: Handling a loaded firearm inside a residence during a domestic dispute and pointing it at a household member is not a lawful act performed with due care.
•	Positive Eyewitness Testimonies: Eyewitness accounts of the children categorically established that the shooting was deliberate and preceded by aggressive overt acts (kicking the victim, retrieving the firearm, and aiming it), negating any claim that the discharge was accidental or devoid of criminal intent. 

Ratio Decidendi
Accident under Article 12(4) of the Revised Penal Code presupposes the complete absence of criminal intent and negligence, requiring that the injury be produced while the accused is engaged in a lawful act performed with due care.
Where the physical evidence—specifically, a direct frontal gunshot wound to the forehead bearing close-range gunpowder burns—contradicts the claim of an accidental discharge, the defense of accident cannot prosper. Coupled with positive, credible eyewitness accounts establishing that the accused retrieved a loaded shotgun following an altercation, aimed it at the victim, and pulled the trigger, the shooting is an intentional felonious killing rather than a fortuitous mishap. Consequently, the exempting circumstance of accident does not lie. 
Fallo:
The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeals sustaining the conviction of Roy San Gaspar by the Regional Trial Court of Isulan, Sultan Kudarat, Branch 19, for parricide with the penalty of reclusion perpetua, subject to the modification of the awarded damages (ordering the payment of civil indemnity, moral damages, and temperate damages, along with legal interest).
Q: In the cases of Roy San Gaspar and Isaias Castillo, why did the Supreme Court reject their defense of accident?

In both People v. Roy San Gaspar (G.R. No. 180496, April 2, 2014) and People v. Isaias Castillo (G.R. No. 172695, June 29, 2007), the Supreme Court rejected the defense of accident because neither accused satisfied the four mandatory requisites under Article 12, paragraph 4 of the Revised Penal Code:
1.	The accused was performing a lawful act;
2.	With due care;
3.	He caused injury to another by mere accident; and
4.	Without fault or intention of causing it.

The Court held that the defense failed in each case for the following specific reasons:

1. In People v. Roy San Gaspar: Deliberate Intent and Physical Evidence Contradicted "Accident"
Roy claimed that his shotgun discharged purely by accident when he pushed the bedroom door open. The Court rejected this claim because:
•	Incompatibility with Forensic/Physical Evidence: The victim suffered a direct, frontal gunshot wound to the left forehead surrounded by gunpowder tattooing and burns inflicted at close range (within six feet). This physical trajectory proved a direct, level shot aimed at the victim, flatly contradicting the narrative of a spontaneous, downward, or accidental discharge caused by hitting a door.
•	Positive Eyewitness Account of Intentional Acts: The children who witnessed the crime testified that prior to the shooting, Roy kicked his wife, went upstairs to retrieve a loaded shotgun, returned, lit a lamp to illuminate the target, aimed the gun, and pulled the trigger.
•	Absence of a Lawful Act Performed with Due Care: Arming oneself with a loaded firearm during a heated domestic squabble and aiming it at an unarmed, sleeping spouse is neither a lawful act nor one performed with due care.

2. In People v. Isaias Castillo: Drawing a Weapon in a Fight Is Unlawful and Negates Due Care
Isaias claimed that the gun discharged accidentally while he and the victim were grappling for possession after the victim grabbed his neck. The Court rejected this defense because:
•	The Act Was Not Lawful: Responding to a verbal argument or a challenge to a fistfight by drawing a concealed gun is not a lawful act. Drawing a deadly weapon in the middle of a street altercation constitutes an unlawful and aggressive act.
•	Lack of Due Care: Pulling out a loaded firearm in the middle of a physical confrontation is inherently reckless and directly invites a struggle for the weapon. The resulting discharge was a foreseeable outcome of introducing a deadly weapon into a fight, not a blameless, fortuitous mishap.
•	Failure of the First Two Requisites: Because Isaias was neither performing a lawful act nor exercising due care when the gun discharged, the first two indispensable elements of Article 12(4) were absent, which automatically barred the application of the exempting circumstance.
