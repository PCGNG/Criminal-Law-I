### Facts
•	The Parties: Manolita Gonzales vda. de Carungcong owned valuable real estate properties in Tagaytay. Her son-in-law was William Sato (a Japanese national), who was married to Manolita's daughter, Zenaida. Unfortunately, Zenaida predeceased her mother. 
•	The Alleged Fraud: Manolita was elderly and blind. William Sato allegedly deceived her into signing and thumbmarking a Special Power of Attorney (SPA) under the pretext that it was related to taxes. In truth, the SPA authorized Sato’s minor daughter to sell Manolita's Tagaytay properties. Sato subsequently had the properties sold, falsified the considerations on the deeds of absolute sale, and pocketed millions in proceeds without accounting for them to Manolita. 
•	The Legal Challenge: After Manolita’s death, her estate (represented by administratrix Mediatrix Carungcong) filed a complaint for estafa. Following revisions, an Information was eventually pushed forward. Sato filed a motion to quash, arguing that as a son-in-law (relative by affinity) of the victim, he was exempt from criminal liability under Article 332 of the Revised Penal Code. 
•	Lower Court Rulings: The Regional Trial Court (RTC) granted the motion to quash and dismissed the case, ruling that the son-in-law/mother-in-law relationship by affinity survived Zenaida’s death, making Sato eligible for the exemption. The Court of Appeals (CA) affirmed the dismissal. 

### Issues 
1.	Does the relationship by affinity between a surviving spouse and the blood relatives of the deceased spouse survive the death of the connecting spouse for purposes of Article 332? (Continuing Affinity Doctrine) 
2.	Does the absolutory cause under Article 332 apply when the crime charged is a complex crime (specifically, estafa through falsification of public documents)? 

### Ruling 
The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the decisions of the RTC and CA, and ordered the trial court to proceed with the trial of the case.
•	On Affinity Surviving Death:
The Supreme Court ruled in favor of the "continuing affinity view," holding that the relationship by affinity created by marriage does not terminate or dissolve automatically upon the death of the connecting spouse. The bond between a son-in-law and his mother-in-law remains intact even if his wife has passed away. 
•	The Catch – Inapplicability to Complex Crimes:
Crucially, however, the Court ruled that Article 332 does not apply when the offense committed is a complex crime. 
o	Article 332 exempts an accused from criminal liability (leaving only civil liability) only for the simple crimes of theft, swindling (estafa), or malicious mischief. 
o	In this case, Sato was charged with estafa through falsification of public documents. Falsification is a distinct crime that violates public interest and the integrity of public documents, which transcends mere private family relations. 
o	Because a complex crime is treated by law as a single integrated offense made up of component crimes, it cannot be split up or treated as a simple crime of estafa just to allow the offender to invoke the absolutory cause. Therefore, Sato could not escape criminal prosecution.

### Question
Was the absolutory cause provided under Article 332 of the Revised Penal Code appreciated in favor of the accused? Why?
No, the absolutory cause provided under Article 332 of the Revised Penal Code was not appreciated in favor of the accused (William Sato).
Why was it not appreciated?
While the Supreme Court agreed with the lower courts that the relationship by affinity between the son-in-law and his mother-in-law survived the death of his wife (the connecting spouse), the Court ultimately refused to apply the exemption because Article 332 does not apply to complex crimes.
The reasoning rests on the following legal principles:
•	Strict Application to Simple Crimes Only: Article 332 explicitly limits its coverage to the simple crimes of theft, swindling (estafa), and malicious mischief committed against specific family members.
•	The Nature of the Charge: The accused was not charged with simple estafa. He was charged with a complex crime—estafa through falsification of public documents.
•	Violation of Public Interest: Falsification of a public document is a crime against public interest and faith, as it undermines the integrity of official records. The Supreme Court ruled that when a crime involves a public wrong beyond a purely private property dispute between family members (such as falsification), the rationale behind Article 332 ceases to apply.
•	Indivisibility of a Complex Crime: Under the law, a complex crime is treated as a single, indivisible offense. The Court cannot "split" the charge into a simple crime of estafa just to extend the family exemption to the accused.
Because the crime charged was a complex crime involving falsification, William Sato could not invoke the absolutory cause to escape criminal prosecution.
