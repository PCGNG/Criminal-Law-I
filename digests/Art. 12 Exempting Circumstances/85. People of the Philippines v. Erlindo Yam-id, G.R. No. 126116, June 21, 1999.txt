
Facts 
On April 1, 1994, at around 2:00 o'clock in the afternoon, Julius was sent by his grandmother, Amanda Ceniza, to Brgy. Tutay, Pinamungajan,Cebu, to deliver benignit, a local delicacy, to his aunt Bebing Dequiado. Jerry Tejamo was with Julius. On their way to Dequiado's house, they passed by the house of Erlindo Yam-id who greeted them, "Good Evening". After said salutation, Erlindo Yam-id suddenly unsheathed a long bolo. On instinct, Julius pushed Jerry, who was then walking in front of him, and told the latter to run. Appellant ran after the two. Jerry was overtaken by Julius. Julius momentarily stopped to wait for Jerry, but Erlindo caught up with Jerry. Erlindo Yam-id stabbed Jerry with the bolo on the left portion of his back. Not content, appellant held Jerry by the hair and hacked him on the nape. Jerry fell to the ground. As a coup de grace, appellant stabbed Jerry on the right side of his back. Jerry died on the spot. Appellant then knelt over the prostrate body of Jerry and sucked the blood from his neck.

Julius fled and alerted Jerry’s parents, Danilo and Aniceta Tejamo. Danilo rushed to the scene, whereupon Yam-id attacked him, hacking him on the bridge of his nose and on the head. Danilo collapsed unconscious. Believing Danilo was already dead after Aniceta screamed, Yam-id grabbed Danilo’s collar to deliver a final blow; however, the tip of the bolo accidentally pierced Yam-id's own abdomen. Perturbed and bleeding, Yam-id threw down his bolo and fled to his house. Danilo survived following immediate medical treatment. 

Yam-id was charged in two separate Informations before the Regional Trial Court (RTC) of Toledo City: Murder (for Jerry) and Frustrated Homicide (for Danilo). During trial, Yam-id pleaded not guilty and invoked self-defense, claiming that Danilo had shot him in the abdomen and that he merely defended himself, denying any involvement in Jerry’s death. 

The RTC rejected his self-defense claim, convicted him of Murder (imposing the Death penalty under Republic Act No. 7659 due to treachery, evident premeditation, and the minority of the victim) and Frustrated Homicide. On automatic appeal before the Supreme Court, the Public Attorney's Office (PAO) raised the defense of insanity for the first time, citing his macabre act of sucking the child's blood and a belated medical evaluation suggesting schizophrenia. 

Issues 
1.	Whether Yam-id can validly invoke the exempting circumstance of insanity for the first time on appeal. NO

2.	Whether the defense proved by clear and convincing evidence that Yam-id was legally insane at the time of the commission of the crimes. NO

3.	Whether the killing of Jerry Tejamo was attended by treachery and evident premeditation to justify the penalty of death. 

4.	What stage of execution was reached in the assault on Danilo Tejamo (Frustrated vs. Attempted Homicide).

Ruling 
1. On Raising Insanity for the First Time on Appeal: NO.
Insanity is a defense in the nature of confession and avoidance, and as such must be proved beyond reasonable doubt (People vs. Ambal, 100 SCRA 35 [1980]). In considering the plea of insanity as a defense in a criminal prosecution, the starting premise is that the law presumes all persons to be of sound mind, or otherwise stated, the law takes for granted that acts are done consciously. Insanity being the exception rather than the rule in the human condition, "the moral and legal presumption is that freedom and intelligence constitute the normal condition of a person and that a felonious or criminal act (delicto deloso) has been done with deliberate intent, that is, with freedom, intelligence and malice" and that whoever, therefore, invokes insanity as a defense has the burden of proving its existence.

In the case at bar, the defense regrettably failed to discharge its burden of proving that accused-appellant was insane at the time of the commission of the crime. The only intimation of insanity that accused-appellant could point at is the non-medical opinion of the PAO that accused-appellant was suffering from schizophrenia because he sipped his victim's blood and tried to kill himself afterwards. No medical certificate was presented to substantiate the claim of insanity. No testimony was proffered to support the allegation. We are not aware that the PAO now has the expertise, more so the authority, to diagnose its clients of their mental condition. While we agree that sucking Jerry's blood and stabbing one's self in the stomach are not acts expected of a normal person, we, however, have to be careful in distinguishing between insanity and passion or eccentricity, mental weakness or mere depression resulting from some physical ailment. The State should guard against sane murderers escaping punishment through a general plea of insanity

The Supreme Court ruled that Yam-id could not change theories on appeal. During trial, his sole defense was self-defense and denial, which are inherently inconsistent with insanity. Having failed to plead or present an iota of evidence establishing insanity before the trial court, the belated defense on appeal was rejected. Verily, the defense of insanity was not even raised during the trial of the case. It is invoked only now on appeal, giving us the impression that it is but an afterthought.

2. On the Apparent "Bizarreness" and Insanity Defense: NO.
Gruesome, heinous, or unnatural acts—such as sucking the victim's blood—do not ipso facto establish legal insanity. Insanity under Philippine law requires a complete deprivation of intelligence and free will immediately prior to or at the moment of the crime. 

•	Yam-id’s conscious actions immediately after the stabbing—fleeing to his house upon accidentally wounding himself and hiding—demonstrated a clear sense of self-preservation and an awareness of the wrongfulness of his acts.

•	Post-factum clinical speculation without proof of total deprivation of reason at the time of the offense is insufficient to overcome the presumption of sanity.

3. On the Attendant Circumstances and Penalty for Murder:
•	Treachery was present: The victim was a defenseless six-year-old child. Under established jurisprudence, the killing of a child of tender years by an adult is inherently characterized by treachery, as the child is utterly incapable of defending himself. 
•	Evident Premeditation was absent: The mere existence of a prior land dispute between the families does not prove that Yam-id planned the killing, clung to a determined resolution to kill, or had sufficient time to reflect on the consequences of the act. 
•	Penalty: Because treachery qualified the killing to Murder, but evident premeditation was unproven and no other aggravating circumstances existed, the mandatory death penalty was reduced to reclusion perpetua. 

4. On the Crime Committed Against Danilo Tejamo: Attempted Homicide, not Frustrated. 
The medical certificate showed that Danilo suffered hack wounds on the nasal area and scalp that required only 10 to 12 days to heal, and the attending physician confirmed the wounds were not mortal. Under Article 6 of the RPC, for a felony to be frustrated, the offender must perform all acts of execution that would produce the felony as a consequence, but which nevertheless do not produce it due to causes independent of the perpetrator's will. Because the wounds were non-fatal, the offense did not reach the frustrated stage; Yam-id was guilty only of Attempted Homicide. 

Dispositive Portion 
The Supreme Court MODIFIED the RTC judgment: 
1.	In Criminal Case No. TCS-2381 (for the killing of Jerry Tejamo), Erlindo Yam-id was found GUILTY of MURDER and sentenced to reclusion perpetua (reduced from Death), and ordered to pay civil indemnity and actual damages. 
2.	In Criminal Case No. TCS-2382 (for the assault on Danilo Tejamo), he was found GUILTY of ATTEMPTED HOMICIDE (downgraded from Frustrated Homicide) and sentenced to an indeterminate penalty of 2 months and 1 day of arresto mayor, as minimum, to 2 years and 4 months of prision correccional, as maximum. 

Why did the Supreme Court reject Erlindo Yam-id’s defense of insanity?

1. Inadmissibility of Changing Theory for the First Time on Appeal
Under Philippine remedial law, an appellant cannot adopt a completely new defense theory on appeal that was never raised, pleaded, or tried before the lower court.
•	At trial, Yam-id’s sole defense was denial and self-defense—he claimed that Danilo shot him and that he merely fought back, while outright denying having killed the six-year-old Jerry.
•	Insanity is a defense in the nature of confession and avoidance: it admits the performance of the act while pleading exemption from liability due to a lack of mental agency.
•	Having maintained that he acted in lawful self-defense throughout the trial, he was judicially estopped from pivoting to insanity before the Supreme Court.

2. Failure to Discharge the Heavy Burden of Proof
The law presumes every person to be sane (Article 800, Civil Code; Article 12(1), Revised Penal Code). To successfully claim exemption by reason of insanity, the accused must overcome this presumption with clear and convincing evidence.
•	The defense failed to present any medical, psychiatric, or psychological evidence contemporaneous with the crime showing that Yam-id suffered from a mental disease that completely deprived him of reason.
•	A belated, post-conviction medical report submitted on appeal was rejected because it did not prove his state of mind at the exact moment of the killing.

3. Gruesome or Bizarre Acts Do Not Equate to Legal Insanity
The defense argued that Yam-id’s horrific act—kneeling over the child’s body to suck blood from the neck—was inherently the conduct of an insane mind. The Supreme Court flatly rejected this premise:

•	Legal insanity requires complete deprivation of intelligence, not moral depravity. An act that is unusually cruel, beastly, eccentric, or abhorrent does not automatically mean the perpetrator is legally insane under Article 12(1) of the RPC.
•	Demeanor proved discernment and self-preservation: Immediately after accidentally impaling his own abdomen with his bolo, Yam-id threw down his weapon, fled to his house, and hid. The Court underscored that this instinct of self-preservation and evasion of capture showed he was conscious of his environment, felt physical pain, recognized danger, and understood that his actions were wrong and carried consequences.
