### FACTS
Wilfredo lived in the house of his father, Bernardo. Bernardo complained to his daughters (Elvira and Emelinda) that Wilfredo frequently caused trouble whenever he was drunk and expressed his intention to transfer Wilfredo to separate quarters. When their father went to his room, Wilfredo, who looked drunk because he was red in the face, ran to the kitchen and got two (2) knives and then went inside their father's room. Wilfredo stabbed Bernardo. The autopsy report showed that the victim had ten (10) stab wounds on various parts of his body. Cause of death: stab wound in the heart.

When charged with parricide, Wilfredo interposed the defense of insanity to exempt himself from criminal liability. In support thereof, an expert psychiatrist from the National Center for Mental Health testified that Wilfredo was diagnosed with schizophrenia twenty days after the incident based on history and examinations. On cross-examination, however, the psychiatrist admitted that he could only presume Wilfredo's mental condition at the exact time of the stabbing and conceded that persons suffering from such disorder experience lucid intervals.

On cross-examination, Elvira stated that accused-appellant had been staying in their father's house for four (4) years after accused-appellant separated from his wife; that in 1988 accused-appellant was confined at the Bicutan Rehabilitation Center in Taguig, Metro Manila for addiction to gasoline; that he had been discharged from the same a long time ago, although she could not remember the year he was discharged; that accused-appellant had not shown any indication that he was crazy although he was also treated at the Baguio General Hospital for addiction to gasoline. 

### ISSUE
Did the exempting circumstance of insanity under Article 12, paragraph 1 of the Revised Penal Code exempt Wilfredo from criminal liability? NO. The defense of insanity has no merit.

### HELD & DOCTRINE
(Verbatim Legal Basis)

"Art. 12 of the Revised Penal Code provides:
Art. 12. Circumstances which exempt from criminal liability. — The following are exempt from criminal liability:
1.	An imbecile or an insane person, unless the latter has acted during a lucid interval.
x x x x

In People v. Formigones, it was held:

The Supreme Court of Spain held that in order that this exempting circumstance may be taken into account, it is necessary that there be a complete deprivation of intelligence in committing the act, that is, that the accused will have no responsibility for the cause, will be completely devoid of reason, of will, and will commit the crime in the absence of free determination and discernment, which is the cause and foundation of criminal liability; for where there is no consciousness and liberty of action, there can be no crime, but rather a simple physical act; and this condition can only be made manifest when there is a complete deprivation of intelligence, whether that intelligence be diseased or healthy. 


In People v. Rafanan, Jr.: 
A linguistic or grammatical analysis of those standards suggests that Formigones established two (2) distinguishable tests: 
(a) the test of cognition — "complete deprivation of intelligence in committing the [criminal] act," and 

(b) the test of volition — "or that there be a total deprivation of freedom of the will." But our caselaw shows common reliance on the test of cognition, 

rather than oil a test relating to "freedom of the will;" examination of our caselaw has failed to turn up any case where this Court has exempted an accused on the sole ground that he was totally deprived of "freedom of the will," i.e., without an accompanying "complete deprivation of intelligence." This is perhaps, to be expected since a person's volition naturally reaches out only towards that which is presented as desirable by his intelligence, whether that intelligence be diseased or healthy.

Accused-appellant must thus prove that he was completely deprived of reason when he killed his father in order to be considered exempt from criminal liability."

However, this has not been shown in this case. There is nothing either in the report of Dr. Gerona or in his testimony which indubitably show that accused-appellant was completely without reason on the night of August 14, 1994 when he killed his father because the latter wanted him to leave the house. Although he said that in his opinion accused-appellant was schizophrenic when he committed the crime, and that he was 99% certain of this, he was later less certain when questioned by the trial judge and admitted that accused-appellant was mentally well at least after his discharge from the Bicutan Rehabilitation Center in 1988 and for some time until he was confined at the mental, hospital in 1994, after the commission of the crime.

### RATIO
To successfully invoke the exempting circumstance of insanity under Article 12(1) of the Revised Penal Code, the defense must establish with clear and convincing evidence that there was a complete deprivation of intelligence, reason, and discernment at the very moment of the commission of the crime.
Medical diagnosis showing that an accused suffers from schizophrenia is insufficient where the examining physician can merely presume the mental condition at the time of the killing and admits that the illness is characterized by lucid intervals. Since insanity is an affirmative defense in the nature of confession and avoidance, the accused bears the burden of overcoming the presumption of sanity by proving total deprivation of will and consciousness contemporaneous with the criminal act. Having failed to prove that he acted without discernment when he stabbed the victim, Wilfredo cannot claim exemption from criminal liability.

### FALLO
"WHEREFORE, the decision of the Regional Trial Court is AFFIRMED with the MODIFICATION that accused-appellant is sentenced to suffer the penalty of reclusion perpetua. No costs.
SO ORDERED."

### QUESTION
Explain why the Supreme Court rejected the accused’s defense of insanity despite the accused’s admission to a mental hospital prior to the commission of the crime and even thereafter.
1. Proof Must Relate to the Exact Time of the Commission of the Crime
Under Philippine criminal law, every person is presumed sane, and insanity is an affirmative defense in the nature of confession and avoidance. To be exempting, the defense must establish with clear and convincing evidence that the accused was suffering from a complete deprivation of intelligence, reason, and discernment at the very moment or immediately preceding the execution of the crime. 
The Court emphasized that hospital confinements, psychiatric observations, or diagnostic histories occurring prior or subsequent to the crime do not establish that the accused was insane at the exact moment he committed the felonious act. 

2. The Nature of Schizophrenia and Lucid Intervals
The defense's expert witness, Dr. Rico Angelo Gerona III of the National Center for Mental Health (NCMH), diagnosed the accused with schizophrenia. However, the Court underscored critical weaknesses in the expert’s findings: 
•	Mere Presumption: The psychiatrist did not examine or treat the accused prior to or during the stabbing; he only evaluated him when he was admitted to the NCMH weeks after the crime. Consequently, when questioned by the trial court, the expert conceded that his assessment of the accused’s state of mind at the precise time of the killing was merely a presumption. 
•	Occurrence of Lucid Intervals: Schizophrenia is characterized by remissions and lucid intervals. A clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia before or after an offense does not negate the strong probability that the offender acted during a lucid interval. The law explicitly provides that an insane person remains criminally liable if the act was committed during a lucid interval (Art. 12[1], RPC). 

3. Presence of a Clear Motive and Rational Behavior
The surrounding circumstances and the accused's conduct belied a mind completely deprived of reason: 
•	Identifiable Motive: The evidence showed that the victim (Wilfredo’s father) had complained about Wilfredo's drunk behavior and planned to evict him to separate quarters. Upon stabbing his father, Wilfredo exclaimed: "Pinalalayas mo ako!" ("You are driving me away!"). This established that his act was not an irrational, blind impulse devoid of consciousness, but an act provoked by resentment and revenge. 
•	Remorse and Awareness: When examined at the mental facility, the accused recounted the circumstances of the killing, recognized the reason behind it (his father driving him out), and expressed remorse and the hope to be forgiven. Such emotional and rational responses demonstrate awareness of the gravity and wrongful nature of his act. 
Summary of Controlling Principle
Mere abnormality of mental faculties or prior/subsequent confinement in a mental institution does not automatically equate to legal insanity. Because the defense failed to prove that the accused was totally deprived of free will and intelligence at the exact moment of the stabbing, the statutory presumption of sanity prevailed.
