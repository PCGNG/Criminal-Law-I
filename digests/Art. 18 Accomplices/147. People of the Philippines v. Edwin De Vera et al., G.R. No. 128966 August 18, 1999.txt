### **Case Brief: People of the Philippines v. Edwin De Vera, et al.**

*G.R. No. 128966, August 18, 1999*

---

### **Facts**

One afternoon, Kenneth Florendo (Kenneth), Roderick Garcia (Roderick), Bernardino Medina (Bernardino), and Edwin De Vera (De Vera) rode together in a white car driven by De Vera. After picking up Frederick Capulong (Frederick), they proceeded to the residence of Frederick's ex-girlfriend, Lois. While Kenneth, Roderick, and Bernardino alighted and forced Lois into the backseat of the car, De Vera remained inside the vehicle at the driver's seat.

De Vera then drove the group to a remote, grassy area in Marikina. Upon arrival, Kenneth, Roderick, and Bernardino brought Lois out of the car and fatally shot her, while De Vera stayed inside the car as a lookout. Afterward, De Vera drove his companions away from the scene.

The RTC convicted De Vera as a principal by conspiracy for the crime of Murder, prompting his appeal to the SC, where he contended that he did not join any agreement to kill Lois and merely drove the car and waited inside it.

---

### **Issue**

Is Edwin De Vera criminally liable as a principal by conspiracy, or merely as an accomplice under the Revised Penal Code, for the murder committed by his companions while he waited inside the car as a lookout?

---

### **Ruling**

**Accomplice only.** The Supreme Court held that where the evidence fails to establish beyond reasonable doubt that an accused participated in the criminal resolution or plan to kill the victim, and his involvement was limited to driving the vehicle and standing by as a lookout without performing an act indispensable to the commission of the murder, any doubt as to the degree of his criminal participation must be resolved in favor of the milder form of responsibility, rendering him liable merely as an accomplice under Article 18 of the Revised Penal Code.

**Legal Basis / Doctrines Relied Upon:**

* **Article 8, Revised Penal Code** (Conspiracy and proposal to commit felony)
* **Article 17, Revised Penal Code** (Principals)
* **Article 18, Revised Penal Code** (Accomplices)
* **Article 248, Revised Penal Code** (Murder qualified by treachery)
* *People v. Tamayo*, 44 Phil. 38 (1922) (Distinction between conspirators and accomplices; resolution of doubt in favor of the milder form of liability)
* *People v. Doble*, 114 SCRA 131 (1982) (Criminal liability of a driver/lookout who did not join the original criminal design)

---

### **Ratio Decidendi**

Conspiracy requires proof beyond reasonable doubt that the accused participated in the criminal resolution and decided to commit the felony; when doubt exists as to whether a participant acted as a co-conspirator or a mere accomplice, the doubt must be resolved in favor of the milder form of criminal liability. An accused who did not conceive or join the original plan to kill, and whose overt acts were confined to driving the perpetrators to the scene and acting as a non-indispensable lookout inside the car while his companions executed the victim outside, does not become a principal under Article 17, but is criminally accountable as an accomplice under Article 18 of the Revised Penal Code because he cooperated with knowledge of the criminal design through simultaneous, efficacious, yet dispensable acts.

---

### **Fallo**

The Supreme Court **MODIFIED** the judgment of the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, finding accused-appellant **Edwin De Vera guilty beyond reasonable doubt merely as an ACCOMPLICE to the crime of Murder**, sentencing him to suffer an indeterminate prison term of **eight (8) years and one (1) day of *prision mayor*, as minimum, to fourteen (14) years, eight (8) months, and one (1) day of *reclusion temporal*, as maximum**, and ordering him to indemnify the heirs of the victim in accordance with the modified civil liability apportionment for an accomplice.

---

### **Elaborate Answers to Review Questions**

**1. When is a lookout deemed an accomplice and when a conspirator?**

Whether a lookout is held criminally liable as a **conspirator (principal)** or merely as an **accomplice** depends on the timing of his knowledge, his participation in the decision to commit the crime, and the indispensability of his role:

* **When a Lookout is Deemed a Conspirator (Principal under Articles 8 and 17):**
* He was part of the **prior agreement or criminal resolution**—meaning he participated in planning and deciding that the felony should be committed, and standing guard was his assigned task in their common design; OR
* Even without proof of a prior meeting, his act of standing guard at the scene was **indispensable** to the consummation of the offense (such that the crime could not have been accomplished without his watchful presence, making him a principal by indispensable cooperation under Article 17[3]), or he actively intervened during the assault to prevent rescue or block the victim's escape.


* **When a Lookout is Deemed an Accomplice (Article 18):**
* He had **no part in the original criminal resolution** to commit the felony, having learned of the principals' unlawful intent only after they had already decided to execute it; AND
* He merely concurred in their criminal design by supplying **previous or simultaneous moral or material aid** (such as waiting in a vehicle or keeping watch) that facilitated the execution or escape, **without which the crime could still have been consummated** by the principals on their own.
* Whenever the prosecution's evidence leaves reasonable doubt as to whether a lookout joined the original conspiracy or merely lent simultaneous assistance, settled jurisprudence mandates resolving the doubt in favor of **accomplice** liability.



---

**2. What is the distinction between a conspirator and an accomplice?**

Under **Articles 8, 17, and 18 of the Revised Penal Code**, as explained by the Supreme Court in *De Vera* (citing *People v. Tamayo*), conspirators and accomplices share a **community of design** (both know of and concur with the criminal purpose), but differ fundamentally in three respects:

| Point of Distinction | Conspirator (Principal) | Accomplice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Participation in the Decision** | **Decides** that the crime should be committed; conceives and resolves upon the criminal design alongside the other principals. | **Does not decide** whether the crime should be committed; merely assents to a pre-existing plan already decided upon by the principals. |
| **2. Role in the Crime** | Acts as an **author** of the crime; the act of one is imputed as the act of all, regardless of the specific degree of physical participation. | Acts merely as an **instrument** or auxiliary who performs a subordinate, non-essential role in aiding the principals. |
| **3. Necessity of the Act** | His participation is either direct execution, direct inducement, or **indispensable cooperation** (without which the crime would not have been accomplished). | Cooperates by previous or simultaneous acts that are efficacious and helpful, yet **not indispensable** to the consummation of the offense. |

---

**3. Why is the accused De Vera only an accomplice?**

The Supreme Court downgraded Edwin De Vera's conviction from a principal by conspiracy to an **accomplice** based on three key factual and legal considerations:

1. **Lack of Proof That He Joined the Decision to Kill:**
There was no evidence showing that De Vera participated in any meeting or agreement with Kenneth, Roderick, and Bernardino to murder Lois. Although he was seen riding in the car with them earlier that day, mere companionship or presence does not prove that he was privy to or part of a premeditated plot to execute the victim.
2. **Knowledge and Concurrence Shown by His Conduct During the Abduction and Killing:**
De Vera could not claim complete innocence, however. When his companions forcibly dragged Lois into the backseat of the car he was driving, and when he drove them to an isolated, grassy area in Marikina and waited inside the vehicle while they brought her out and shot her, De Vera plainly became aware of their criminal design and intentionally lent his cooperation by providing transportation and standing by as a lookout.
3. **His Act Was Not Indispensable to the Commission of the Murder:**
Kenneth, Roderick, and Bernardino were armed and physically overpowered and shot Lois entirely on their own outside the car. De Vera's act of remaining inside the car as a lookout and driving them away facilitated the commission of the offense and their subsequent escape, but it was **not an act without which the murder could not have been accomplished**. Because his participation fell short of proving a prior agreement to kill or indispensable cooperation, the Supreme Court resolved the doubt in favor of the milder form of responsibility and convicted him solely as an **accomplice to murder**.
