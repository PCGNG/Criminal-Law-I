### Facts:
Ignas, an elementary graduate and who operates a bakery,  learned from Romenda, a close friend of his wife Wilma Grace, supported by letters, that his wife had been carrying on an extramarital affair with  Nemesio Lopate and that the two had stayed together in  Dangwa inn before she left to work in Taiwan. Upon learning of the illicit liaison, Ignas became furious and declared that there would come a day when he would kill Lopate.

Wilma Grace used to be the cashier of Windfield Enterprise, which is owned by Pauline Gumpic. Pauline had a brother, Nemesio Lopate, whom June Ignas fatally shot. 

About two weeks later, Lopate was fatally shot at a vegetable trading post. Witnesses placed Ignas near the scene concealing a handgun, and Ignas subsequently disclosed to an acquaintance that he had just shot his wife’s paramour. Ignas then went to another province. Days later, when investigating police officers went there to invite him for questioning, Ignas returned with them to the police station, though he denied the killing and interposed an alibi.

Convicted of homicide, Ignas argued that even if found criminally liable, he was entitled to the mitigating circumstances of immediate vindication of a grave offense, passion or obfuscation, and voluntary surrender.

### Issue: Is the appellant entitled to benefit from any mitigating circumstance?

### Ruling: 

Appellant points out that the victim’s act of maintaining an adulterous relationship with appellant’s wife constituted a grave offense to his honor, not to mention the shame, anguish, and anxiety he was subjected to. Even the mere sight of the victim must have triggered an uncontrollable emotional outburst on appellant’s part, so that even a chance meeting caused in him an irresistible impulse powerful enough to overcome all reason and restraint. Secondly, appellant points out that the trial court failed to consider his voluntary surrender as a mitigating circumstance. 

Appellant became aware of the treatment offensive to his dignity as a husband and to the peace and tranquility of his home two weeks earlier.

Hence, we agree with the Solicitor General that the lapse of two (2) weeks between his discovery of his wife’s infidelity and the killing of her supposed paramour could no longer be considered proximate. The passage of a fortnight is more than sufficient time for appellant to have recovered his composure and assuaged the unease in his mind. The established rule is that there can be no immediate vindication of a grave offense when the accused had sufficient time to recover his serenity.[112] Thus, in this case, we hold that the mitigating circumstance of immediate vindication of a grave offense cannot be considered in appellant’s favor.

We likewise find the alleged mitigating circumstance of passion and obfuscation inexistent. The rule is that the mitigating circumstances of vindication of a grave offense and passion and obfuscation cannot be claimed at the same time, if they arise from the same facts or motive. In other words, if appellant attacked his victim in proximate vindication of a grave offense, he could no longer claim in the same breath that passion and obfuscation also blinded him. Moreover, for passion and obfuscation to be well founded, the following requisites must concur: (1) there should be an act both unlawful and sufficient to produce such condition of mind; and (2) the act which produced the obfuscation was not far removed from the commission of the crime by a considerable length of time, during which the perpetrator might recover his moral equanimity. To repeat, the period of two (2) weeks which spanned the discovery of his wife’s extramarital dalliance and the killing of her lover was sufficient time for appellant to reflect and cool off.
### Question: 
In the case of June Ignas, why is he not entitled to the mitigating circumstances of immediate vindication of a grave offense, passion and obfuscation, and voluntary surrender?
Two full weeks had elapsed from the time Ignas discovered the love letters and learned of his wife’s extramarital affair with the victim (Nemesio Lopate) to the night of the fatal shooting. The Court held that this two-week gap was more than enough time for him to cool off, reflect, and recover his normal composure and equanimity. Hence, the vindication could no longer be considered "immediate" or proximate in law.

Ignas did not spontaneously present himself to the police station to own up to the act or spare authorities the trouble of tracking him down. Instead, he left the province and went to Kayapa, Nueva Vizcaya. He only returned to Benguet after a team of police investigators went to Nueva Vizcaya specifically to invite him for questioning, and upon arriving, he flatly denied having any role in the killing. The Court ruled that yielding only when pursued and located by law enforcement, while maintaining complete denial, does not meet the legal standard of voluntary surrender.
