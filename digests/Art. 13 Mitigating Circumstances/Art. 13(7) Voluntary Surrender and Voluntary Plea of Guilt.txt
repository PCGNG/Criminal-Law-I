If both voluntary surrender and voluntary plea of guilt are present in the problem, these two will always and always give rise to two separate and distinct mitigating circumstances.
### PEOPLE v. IGNAS G.R. 140514-15, SEPTEMBER 20, 2003, QUISUMBING, J 
### FACTS: Wilma and June Ignas were married. Romenda, however, informed June about the extramarital affair between Wilma and Nemesio. June became furious and declared that he is going to kill Nemesio. One evening thereafter, June shot Nemesio resulting to his death. June was charged with the crime of Murder.  
### ISSUE: W/N immediate vindication of a grave offense, passion and obfuscation, and voluntary surrender should be appreciated (NO)  
### RULING: NO.  
*Immediate vindication of a grave offense Passion and obfuscation*
The rule is that the mitigating circumstances of vindication of a grave offense and passion and obfuscation cannot be claimed at the same time, if they arise from the same facts or motive. In other words, if appellant attacked his victim in proximate vindication of a grave offense, he could no longer claim in the same breath that passion and obfuscation also blinded him.  
Moreover, for passion and obfuscation to be well founded, the following requisites must concur: (i) there should be an act both unlawful and sufficient to produce such condition of mind; and (ii) the act which produced the obfuscation was not far removed from the commission of the crime by a considerable length of time, during which the perpetrator might recover his moral equanimity. 
Here, the period of two (2) weeks which spanned the discovery of his wife's extramarital dalliance and the killing of her lover was sufficient time for appellant to reflect and cool off.

*Voluntary surrender*
For voluntary surrender to be appreciated, the following requirements must be satisfied: (i) the offender has not actually been arrested; (ii) the offender surrendered himself to a person in authority; and (iii) the surrender was voluntary.  
Records show, however, that leaflets and posters were circulated for information to bring the killer of Nemesio to justice. A team of police investigators from La Trinidad, Benguet then went to Kayapa, Nueva Vizcaya to invite appellant for questioning. Only then did he return to Benguet. But he denied the charge of killing the victim. Clearly, appellant's claimed surrender was neither spontaneous nor voluntary.
### NOTE
On the other hand, if par. 4 - sufficient provocation, par. 5- immediate vindication of a grave offense, and par. 6 - sudden impulse of passion and obfuscation are present in the commission of the crime and they arose from the same facts and circumstances, the SC said, in the cases of People v. Ignas and People v. Pagal, that all these three (3) shall be appreciated only as one mitigating circumstance.  
Therefore, if these three (3) arose from the same facts and circumstances, they shall be considered only as one mitigating circumstance. However, if voluntary surrender and voluntary plea of guilt are both present, they shall always be considered as two separate and distinct mitigating circumstances because voluntary surrender and voluntary plea of guilt will never arise from the same facts and circumstances.

### Elements of voluntary surrender
1. That the offender has not been actually arrested; 
2. That he surrendered to a person in authority or his agent; and 
3. The said surrender must be done voluntarily.

*First element - That the offender has not been actually arrested*
Even if there is already a warrant of arrest issued by the court, if the police officers have not yet exerted any effort to arrest the said accused or offender, if he has not yet been actually arrested and he voluntarily went to the police to give himself up, that would still amount to voluntary surrender.

*Second element - That he surrendered to a person in authority or his agent*
1. A person in authority 
      a. Judge 
      b. Mayor   
      c. Governor 
      d. Barangay chairman  
2. Agents of persons in authority a. Police officers b. Barangay tanods
      a. Police Officers
      b. Barangay Tanods

*Third element - The surrender must be done voluntarily* 
Surrender is said to be done voluntarily if it is done spontaneously and unconditionally. The offender felt remorse and he wanted to plead guilty or he wanted to save the government from the funds, the time, or the efforts that they will exert if they look for him. Surrender is said to be done voluntarily.

### Elements of Voluntary Plea of Guilt
1. That the offender pleaded guilty spontaneously and unconditionally; 
2. The plea of guilt must be done in open court; and 
3. The plea of guilt must be done before the presentation of the evidence for the prosecution.  

*First element - That the offender pleaded guilty spontaneously and unconditionally*
A plea of guilt is said to be done spontaneously when it is on the original crime charged. It is said to be done unconditionally when the plea of guilt is not subject to any condition.  

### Q: 
X was charged with Frustrated Homicide. He pleaded not guilty. During pre-trial, through his counsel, X stated that he wanted to enter into a valid plea-bargaining agreement and he wanted to plead guilty to a lesser crime of Serious Physical Injuries. The court granted the motion of the counsel. Hence, on the next hearing date, X was re-arraigned. This time, the Information charges him with Serious Physical Injuries. He immediately pleaded guilty. After determining the civil aspect of the case, the judge convicted X. In imposing the penalty upon X, the judge did not consider his plea of guilt as a mitigating circumstance. Is the judge correct?  
### A: 
YES. The judge is correct because X's plea of guilt was not done spontaneously. It was not done in the original crime charged of Frustrated Homicide, rather, it was to a lesser crime based on a valid plea-bargaining agreement. Absent that spontaneity, the plea of guilt cannot be considered as a mitigating circumstance.

### Q: 
X was charged as a co-conspirator in the crime of Robbery. He was the only one arrested, all others were in hiding and at large. The Information alleges that X committed the crime with three (3) other John Does.  During arraignment, X pleaded not guilty. During pre- trial, X, through his counsel, moved that he wanted to enter into a plea-bargaining agreement. He wanted to be re-arraigned and to plead guilty as a mere accessory to the crime of Robbery. The judge granted X's motion. He was re-arraigned. He was charged with the same crime of Robbery, but only as an accessory and not as a co-principal and co-conspirator.  The judge rendered his decision finding him guilty of Robbery, but he did not consider his plea of guilt as a mitigating circumstance. Is the judge correct?  ### A: 
YES. The judge is correct. Although the plea of guilt was on the original crime charged of Robbery, it was subject to a condition that he becomes a mere accessory, instead of a co-principal or co-conspirator. Therefore, the said plea of guilt cannot be considered as a mitigating circumstance.

*Second element - The plea of guilt must be done in open court*
It should be done in open court which is the court that tried the case – MTC or RTC, not before the appellate court. 

*Third element – The plea of guilt must be done before the presentation of the evidence for the prosecution*
 It must be done before the presentation of the evidence for the prosecution.  

### Q: 
X was on his way home. He heard the cries of his sister. Upon opening the door, he saw his 7-year-old sister down on the floor, naked, and the neighbor, Y, on top of his sister, who was also naked. When X saw this, he shouted aloud in anger. When Y noticed him, Y immediately jumped out the window and ran away. X was very mad. He got his pistol and chased Y. Thereafter, he repeatedly fired at Y resulting to his death. After committing the act of killing Y, X went into hiding.  Two (2) years later, X voluntarily came out from hiding, and went to the police and gave himself up. X is now being charged with the crime of Homicide.  X raised the justifying circumstance of defense of a relative. Is there defense of a relative or incomplete defense of a relative?  
### A: 
NONE. There is no defense of a relative nor the incomplete defense of a relative because the element of unlawful aggression is absent. The act done by the neighbor on the sister constituted unlawful aggression. However, the moment the neighbor ran away, X should not have followed and killed Y. The moment the neighbor ran away, the unlawful aggression had ceased to exist. At the time Y was killed by X, the unlawful aggression had already ceased. Since unlawful aggression is the element that is absent, there is neither defense of a relative nor the incomplete defense of a relative.

### Q:
What if in the same problem, X raised the mitigating circumstances of (a) immediate vindication of a grave offense, (b) sudden impulse of passion and obfuscation, and (c) voluntary surrender. Are these mitigating circumstances present?  
### A: 
(a) YES. There is immediate vindication of a grave offense. The act done by the neighbor in raping the sister is a grave offense. The act of killing took place immediately.  

(b) YES. There is also a sudden impulse of passion and obfuscation. The act done by the neighbor was an unlawful act sufficient to produce passion and obfuscation on the part of X. There was no lapse of time in between the said act of rape and the act of killing. Therefore, there was also a sudden impulse of passion and obfuscation.  
*NOTE:* However, the judge shall consider them only as one (1) mitigating circumstance in computing the penalty.  

(c) NO. Voluntary Surrender cannot be considered in this case. Two (2) years have lapsed, thus, the element of spontaneity is_absent. Even if after two (2) years, he felt remorse and he wanted to plead guilty, two (2) years is already a long time. It cannot be said that the surrender was done spontaneously because by that time, the Government and police officers have already exerted efforts to look for him based on the warrant of arrest.

### Q: 
What if in the same problem, after killing Y, X immediately went to his uncle who is the Barangay Chairman. X told his uncle what he did and that he was afraid to be placed behind bars. The uncle advised him that he has to surrender and told him, "I will bring you to the police station." The uncle prevailed and X was brought to the police station. Is voluntary surrender a mitigating circumstance in this case?  
### A:
NO. Voluntary surrender is not a mitigating circumstance in this case. When he surrendered to his uncle, there was no intent on his part to surrender voluntarily. His intention was to tell his uncle that he was afraid to be placed behind bars and arrested. It was only his uncle who prevailed that brought him to the police station. Therefore, absent his intent to surrender voluntarily because he felt remorse and he wanted to save the Government from the funds and efforts to be exerted, the act of X cannot be considered as voluntary surrender.

### Q: 
X was found in possession of Shabu. He was charged with violation of Sec. 11 of RA 9165, as amended - Illegal possession of dangerous drugs. He pleaded guilty. The judge rendered a decision finding him guilty beyond reasonable doubt of Illegal Possession of Dangerous Drugs. However, the judge did not appreciate his plea of guilt as a mitigating circumstance. Is the judge correct?  
### A: YES. The judge is correct. His plea of guilt cannot be considered as a mitigating circumstance because what he violated is a special penal law. Generally, in special penal laws, you do not consider modifying circumstances, such as voluntary plea of guilt.  
Another reason is that under Sec. 98 of RA 9165, it expressly provides that the RPC cannot apply to it suppletory. Therefore, since it is the special penal law itself that provides that RPC cannot apply to it, then modifying circumstances cannot be considered, except when the offender is a minor.

### Q: 
X was found in possession of a caliber .38 revolver, a low-powered pistol. He was charged with violation of RA 10591 - Illegal possession of loose firearms. He immediately pleaded guilty. The judge rendered a decision finding him guilty of violation of RA 10591. In imposing the penalty, the judge did not consider his plea of guilt as a mitigating circumstance. Is the judge correct?  
### A:
NO. The judge is wrong. RA 10591 carries the same nomenclature of penalty as that of the RPC - Prision mayor. Therefore, applying Art. 10 of the RPC, the RPC applies suppletory to violations of RA 10591.

### PEOPLE v. MARIANO G.R. NO. 178145 | JULY 7, 2014 | BERSAMIN, J. 
### FACTS: 
De Leon was driving his owner-type jeep, while Mariano was driving his Toyota red pick-up. De Leon and Mariano had a traffic altercation, but was pacified by De Leon's uncle. Thereafter, De Leon decided to go to his mother's house to pick up some items. He parked his car in front of his mother's house and alighted the car. However, he was bumped by the moving vehicle driven by Mariano. De Leon lost consciousness and was brought to the hospital. Mariano was charged with the crime of Frustrated Homicide. Upon review of the CA, the charged of Frustrated Homicide was modified to Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Serious Physical Injuries. Mariano interposed voluntary surrender.
### ISSUE: W/N voluntary surrender should be appreciated (NO) 
### Ruling: 
(NO) The mitigating circumstance of voluntary surrender cannot be appreciated in his favor. The mitigating circumstance of voluntary surrender cannot be appreciated. In the imposition of the penalties, the courts shall have their sound discretion, without regard to the rules prescribed in Art. 64 of the RPC. The rationale behind the law is that in quasi offenses, the carelessness, imprudence, or negligence may vary from one situation to another, in nature, extent, and resulting consequences. In order that there may be a fair and just application of the penalty, the courts must have ample discretion in its imposition, without being bound by what the mathematical formula provided for in Article 64.

### Q: 
X came from a party. He was drunk and driving recklessly. He hit and bump a pedestrian crossing. The man was severely injured. X was afraid, and so, he left immediately. Next morning, X learned in the news that the man he hit was already dead. X, bothered by his conscience, went to the police and confessed that he was the driver of the car that hit and killed the victim. X was charged with Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide. X felt remorse and he was so sorry for what he did. And so, during the arraignment, X pleaded guilty. After determining the civil aspect of the case, the judge convicted X of Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide. However, the judge did not consider the mitigating circumstances of voluntary surrender and voluntary plea of guilt. Is the judge correct?
### A: 
YES. The judge is correct. As held by the SC in the case of People v. Mariano, the case filed against X is a quasi-offense under Title 14, Art. 365. The SC said that as expressly stated in Art. 365(5), in case of quasi- offenses, the court shall impose the penalty based on its sound discretion without the need to appreciate modifying circumstances as provided for under Art. 64 of the RPC.  
So, in imposing the penalty in case of quasi-offenses, courts are not mandated to consider Art. 64 of the RPC because in imposing the penalty, the court shall only be guided by their sound discretion. Whether to appreciate or not these mitigating circumstances, they are not mandated to comply with Art. 64.  
Art. 64 provides for the rules and imposition of divisible penalties by appreciating mitigating and aggravating circumstances.

### Q: 
Public officer X and his subordinates were all charged with the crime of Plunder as conspirators. The case was filed by Office of the Ombudsman before the Sandiganbayan and raffled to the first division.  
The first division found probable cause and therefore, a warrant was issued. They even had a press conference wherein the justices of the first division before national television stated that they already issued a warrant of arrest against X and his subordinates.  
X and his subordinates were watching the television and upon learning that a warrant of arrest has already been issued against them, they immediately went to the Sandiganbayan and surrendered. They were placed behind bars. The crime is not bailable. After trial on the merits, the first division of the SB found X guilty of Plunder. In imposing the penalty, they considered his voluntary surrender as a mitigating circumstance. Are the justices correct in considering the voluntary surrender?  
### A: 
YES. They are correct. The special penal law itself, Sec. 2 of RA 7080 - The Anti-Plunder Act, provides that in imposing the penalty, the court shall consider mitigating or extenuating circumstances. It is the law itself that commands the courts to appreciate mitigating and extenuating circumstances.  NOTE: Only mitigating and extenuating circumstances are included, not aggravating circumstances. Only circumstances that will lower the imposable penalty.
