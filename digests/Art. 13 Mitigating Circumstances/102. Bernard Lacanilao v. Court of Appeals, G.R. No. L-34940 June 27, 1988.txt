### Facts
Lacanilao, a municipal policeman, was on guard duty at the municipal jail when an altercation broke out between detainees Celedonio and Subang over an electric fan. Lacanilao intervened, led Celedonio out of the detention cell, and brought him to the police headquarters desk.
While at the desk, Celedonio threw a punch at Lacanilao, hitting him on the cheek. Insulted and enraged by the prisoner's physical assault upon his person and authority, Lacanilao pulled out his service revolver and fired three successive shots at Celedonio, hitting him in the chest and abdomen. Celedonio died from internal hemorrhage secondary to the gunshot wounds.
Lacanilao surrendered to the authorities and claimed that he was entitled to the mitigating circumstances of sufficient provocation, passion and obfuscation, and lack of intention to commit so grave a wrong.

### Issue
Can the mitigating circumstances of sufficient provocation, passion or obfuscation, and lack of intention to commit so grave a wrong under Article 13 of the Revised Penal Code be credited in favor of Lacanilao?

### Ruling
Only the mitigating circumstances of sufficient provocation and voluntary surrender were appreciated. The SC rejected the claims of passion or obfuscation and lack of intention to commit so grave a wrong.
Verbatim Legal Basis and Quotations from the Supreme Court
"The trial court and the Court of Appeals correctly rejected petitioner's claim of self-defense. While it is true that the victim gave sufficient provocation when he boxed petitioner on the cheek, there was no reasonable necessity for the means employed by petitioner to prevent or repel the aggression."
"The mitigating circumstance of passion or obfuscation cannot be appreciated in favor of petitioner because it is well-settled that passion or obfuscation cannot be considered when the same is generated by the same facts that gave rise to the mitigating circumstance of provocation. The two mitigating circumstances cannot co-exist if they arose from the same cause."
"Neither can the mitigating circumstance of lack of intent to commit so grave a wrong be credited to petitioner. The use of a deadly weapon (a .38 caliber revolver) and the firing of three shots at close range into vital parts of the body of the victim negate the claim that petitioner did not intend to commit so grave a wrong as that committed."

### Ratio Decidendi
1.	Sufficient Provocation vs. Passion or Obfuscation (Article 13[4] and Article 13[6], RPC): While the victim's sudden punch to the face constituted sufficient and immediate provocation, it cannot simultaneously sustain the mitigating circumstance of having acted upon an impulse so powerful as to produce passion or obfuscation. Where both mitigating circumstances originate from one and the same single act or cause—the victim's punch—they cannot be appreciated separately, as doing so would duplicate the mitigation arising from identical facts.

2.	Lack of Intention to Commit so Grave a Wrong (Praeter Intentionem, Article 13[3], RPC): The mitigating circumstance of lack of intent to commit so grave a wrong requires a notable disproportion between the means employed and the resulting consequence. Firing three successive bullets from a deadly weapon (.38 caliber revolver) at close range directed at vital organs (chest and abdomen) inherently demonstrates that the means employed were reasonably sufficient to cause death, thereby negating any assertion of praeter intentionem.
Consequently, with only two mitigating circumstances (sufficient provocation and voluntary surrender) and no aggravating circumstance, the penalty for homicide was properly lowered by one degree pursuant to Article 64, paragraph 5 of the Revised Penal Code.

### Question
Explain why the Supreme held that Bernard Lacanilao is entitled to the privileged mitigating circumstance of justifying circumstance of incomplete fulfillment of duty or lawful exercise of a right under Article 69 of the Revised Penal Code and not as an ordinary mitigating circumstance under Article 13 of the Revised Penal Code.
1. Nature of Article 11, Paragraph 5 as an Incomplete Justifying Circumstance
To claim the justifying circumstance of fulfillment of duty or lawful exercise of a right under Article 11(5) of the RPC, two requisites must concur:
1.	The offender acted in the performance of a duty or in the lawful exercise of a right or office; and
2.	The injury caused or the offense committed was the necessary consequence of the due performance of such duty or the lawful exercise of such right or office.
In Lacanilao's case, he was performing his official duty as a police officer when the incident occurred, thereby satisfying the first condition. However, firing fatal shots exceeded what was necessary to perform that duty, meaning the second requisite was absent.
2. Operation of Article 69 and Inapplicability of the "Majority of Conditions" Requirement
Article 69 of the RPC governs instances where a crime is not wholly excusable because all the conditions required to justify the act (under Article 11) or exempt from criminal liability (under Article 12) are not present.
While Article 69 contains a proviso stating that the penalty reduction applies "provided that the majority of such conditions be present," the Supreme Court clarified that this numerical threshold does not disqualify a claim under Article 11(5):
•	Because fulfillment of duty consists of only two conditions, requiring a mathematical "majority" (which would be more than one, or both) would render Article 69 completely inapplicable to Article 11(5).
•	Under rules of statutory construction, penal statutes are construed liberally in favor of the accused. The legislative intent behind Article 69 is to mitigate punishment when an act is partially justified due to a diminution of criminal perversity or intent.
•	Therefore, the presence of the primary requisite (acting in the performance of duty) is sufficient to invoke Article 69 even if the second condition (necessary consequence) is lacking.
3. Application of the Precedent in People v. Oanis
The Court reiterated the established doctrine in People v. Oanis (74 Phil. 257), which held that where an officer acts in the performance of duty but oversteps the limits of necessity, the circumstance of incomplete fulfillment of duty operates as a privileged mitigating circumstance under Article 69.
4. Legal Effect: Privileged vs. Ordinary Mitigating Circumstance
The distinction is critical to the penalty imposed:
•	Ordinary Mitigating Circumstance (Article 13 in relation to Article 64): An ordinary mitigating circumstance merely affects the periods (minimum, medium, maximum) within the prescribed penalty, or reduces the penalty by one degree only if two or more mitigating circumstances concur without any aggravating circumstance.
•	Privileged Mitigating Circumstance (Article 69): Operates by mandate of law to automatically reduce the penalty by one or two degrees lower than that prescribed by law for the felony, regardless of the ordinary rules of offsetting under Article 64.
Because incomplete justification falls squarely under the special mechanism of Article 69, the Supreme Court lowered the penalty prescribed for the offense by degree rather than merely treating it as a generic factor under Article 13.
